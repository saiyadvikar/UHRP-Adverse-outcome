Title Retrospective Audit of Adverse Maternal Outcomes (Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension, Severe Anaemia, Sickle Cell Disease, and Others) in Women Delivering at District Hospital Khargone: Evaluating the Impact on Improving Future Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes 2. Introduction and Background Adverse pregnancy outcome is a broad term comprising health problems that occur to the mother, the newborn, or both during pregnancy, labour and delivery, and the postpartum period. Some of the common pregnancy complications include Severe anaemia, complications due to Sickle cell Disease, postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), stillbirth, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), obstructed labour, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (Severe PIH, preeclampsia, eclampsia). Thus, women must receive health care before and during pregnancy to decrease the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes . Maternal health remains a critical public health concern in India, particularly in rural and semi-urban areas like Madhya Pradesh, where access to quality antenatal and intrapartum care can be limited. Adverse maternal outcomes, such as pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH, including preeclampsia and eclampsia), severe anaemia, sickle cell disease, and other complications (e.g., postpartum haemorrhage, infections, or gestational diabetes), contribute significantly to maternal morbidity and mortality In Madhya Pradesh, maternal mortality ratios are higher than the national average, with sickle cell disease being prevalent due to regional genetic factors.  (Sinha & Shrivastava, 2019) Retrospective audits of adverse outcomes are established tools in healthcare for identifying substandard care factors, such as delays in diagnosis, inadequate management protocols, or resource gaps. These audits facilitate quality improvement by providing evidence-based insights that inform targeted interventions, such as staff training, protocol revisions, and enhanced referral systems. Studies in India, including retrospective analyses from tertiary centres in Eastern India and nationwide maternal death reviews, demonstrate that audits reveal preventable causes in up to 70-80% of cases, leading to reduced recurrence through feedback loops and policy changes. Globally, perinatal audits have shown to improve outcomes by addressing inequities and optimising care delivery, as seen in facility-based cohort studies in low-resource settings.  (Kumar et al., 2025) This proposal outlines a study to conduct a retrospective audit of adverse outcomes in delivering women at a district hospital, followed by the implementation of improvement processes. The goal is to demonstrate how such audits can prospectively enhance future maternal and neonatal outcomes 3. Rationale and Significance Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR) and Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR) continue to be a major public health issue in Madhya Pradesh, which has one of the country's highest MMRs of 159 per 100,000 live births for 2020-2022. Factors that contribute to this high MMR include Post Partum Hemorrhage (35-40% of total deaths), Hypertensive disorders/Eclampsia (20-25%), Sepsis (15%), and other factors such as anemia and obstructed labour (10-15%), (Meh et al., 2022)  as reported by the Maternal Death Review (MDR) created by the National Health Mission (NHM) and the Sample Registration System (SRS) for 2021-2023. Clinical audits, particularly retrospective ones, enable the identification of patterns in adverse events, contributing factors (e.g., delayed antenatal care, nutritional deficiencies, or sickle cell crises), and substandard care elements (e.g., lack of timely blood transfusions for anaemia). By feeding back these findings into quality improvement cycles—such as the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) model—healthcare teams can implement processes like updated clinical guidelines, staff education, and community outreach. Evidence from similar audits in India shows that reviewing maternal near-misses and deaths leads to better risk stratification and preventive measures, potentially reducing adverse outcomes by 20-30% in subsequent periods. For instance, audits in tertiary hospitals have validated tools like maternal near-miss incidence ratios (e.g., 46.6 per 1,000 live births) to benchmark and improve care. (Chandra et al., 2025) In the context of Madhya Pradesh, where sickle cell disease affects tribal populations and anaemia prevalence exceeds 50% among pregnant women, this intervention addresses local needs. The study will contribute to evidence on audit-driven improvements, potentially scalable to other district hospitals, and support equity in maternal health by focusing on vulnerable groups.  (Bijlwan & Kumar, 2022) 4. Research Question How does the retrospective audit of adverse maternal outcomes (e.g., PIH, severe anaemia, sickle cell disease, and others) in women delivering at a district hospital, followed by targeted quality improvement processes, enhance future maternal and neonatal outcomes? 5. Hypothesis Retrospective audits will identify preventable substandard care factors in adverse outcome cases. Implementation of audit-derived interventions will reduce the incidence of audited adverse outcomes in the post-intervention period compared to baseline. 6. Objectives Primary Objective To assess the impact of a retrospective audit and subsequent quality improvement processes on reducing adverse maternal outcomes in future deliveries. Secondary Objectives To identify substandard care factors, contributing causes, and systemic gaps through the audit. To communicate the audit findings with the respective district and block teams. To evaluate changes in maternal and neonatal outcomes pre- and post-intervention. 7. Methodology 7.1 Study Design This study will be a prospective implementation research study using a repeated retrospective audit and feedback review approach. The intervention involves systematic auditing of adverse pregnancy outcomes followed by structured feedback to health teams and monthly review meetings to assess improvement in care processes and outcomes . 7.2 Study Site The study will be conducted at the District Hospital, Khargone, which serves as the main referral facility for obstetric emergencies in the district. Cases originating from block-level and peripheral health facilities and referred to the District Hospital will be included in the audit. 7.3 Study Population The study population will include women who report to the District Hospital, Khargone, for delivery and experience one or more of the following adverse pregnancy outcomes: Severe anaemia Pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH) Eclampsia Complications related to sickle cell disease Stillbirth Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) In addition, healthcare providers involved in the care pathway of these women—including ASHAs, ANMs, CHOs, Medical Officers, Staff Nurses, and facility managers—will be included as key informants during the audit process. 7.4 Sample Size and Sampling Twenty (20) adverse outcome cases per month will be audited. The total sample size will be 120 cases over a period of six months. Cases will be selected consecutively from eligible women delivering at the District Hospital during the study period. 7.5 Inclusion Criteria All women who delivered (live birth, stillbirth, or abortion >20 weeks) at the hospital during the study period with documented adverse outcomes (PIH, severe anaemia [Hb <7 g/dL], sickle cell disease, postpartum haemorrhage, eclampsia, sepsis, etc.). 7.6 Exclusion Criteria Women referred out before delivery or with incomplete records. 7.7 Study Duration The total duration of the study will be three months, consisting of: Monthly audit of adverse outcome cases Monthly feedback and review meetings Ongoing documentation of changes in care processes 7.8 Data Collection Method (Audit Process) This audit process uses a systematic, multi-source approach to understand adverse outcomes beyond clinical events alone. By combining beneficiary perspectives, provider insights, and record review, the audit identifies actionable gaps and supports continuous learning through regular feedback and review cycles .  Baseline data will be the current data on the pregnant women who delivered in the district hospital, Khargone, in the past 3 months. Conclusion This audit aims to strengthen maternal health services by introducing a structured, routine audit of adverse pregnancy outcomes at District Hospital, Khargone. By systematically reviewing cases of severe anaemia, PIH, eclampsia, sickle cell disease–related complications, stillbirth, and gestational diabetes, and linking audit findings with regular feedback and review meetings, the study focuses on improving how existing services are delivered rather than introducing new clinical interventions. The audit–feedback–review approach is expected to help identify recurring gaps in antenatal care, clinical management, referral practices, and health system responsiveness.
